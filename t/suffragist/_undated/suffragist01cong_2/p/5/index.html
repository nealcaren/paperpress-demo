ANE ADDAMS AND OTHER PROMINENT SUFFRAGISTS ON THEIR WAY TO THE HEARING BEFORE THE HOUSE COMMITTEE

December 6, 1913

BRILLIANT argument for a standing A committee on Woman Suffrage in the House of Representatives was made last week before the House Rules Committee.

A resolution authorizing the creation of a Woman Suffrage Committee in the House has been before the Rules Committee, where it has been under consideration for many months. An informal hearing on the resolution was granted by the Rules Committee last August to the National Council of Women Voters. The only result of these proceedings was the promise of a formal and more extended hearing on the same question during the first week of December. This promise was fulfilled on December 3d, 4th, and 5th.

On Wednesday, December 3d, the National Woman Suffrage Association presented a compact case for the resolution, Dr. Anna Howard Shaw presiding. The antisuffragists, introduced by Mrs. Arthur Dodge, spent Thursday morning, Thursday afternoon, and Friday morning opposing the formation of the committee and denouncing the principle of equal suffrage. On Friday afternoon the Suffragists made a brief but adequate rebuttal under the direction of Mrs. Catherine Waugh McCulloch.

The majority caucus room of the House office building, where the hearings were held, was crowded during the three days of the proceedings, especially on the opening day, when every available chair in the building was brought to the great room, and many people were still left standing.

Jane Addams Speaks The speech of Miss Jane Addams was interrupted by a fire of questions from Congressman Hardwick of Georgia. Miss Addams began by reminding the committee that she had appeared before them last August when they granted a hearing to the National Council of Women Voters, and that at that time Congressman Hardwick had said that the question of altering the electorate was not a suitable subject for discussion in the United States Congress.

“I did not at that time," said Miss Addams, “have the opportunity to answer this statement, and will use the present occasion to show ten instances where Congress has taken the question of suffrage for men into its own hands."

Miss Addams referred first to the Fifteenth Amendment, which she admitted was not a happy instance to quote to a gentleman from Georgia. Other instances were the granting of the vote to Indians when their reservations were broken up; to former Confederate soldiers when they took the oath of allegiance; to the foreigners who fought in the Civil War, and to the men who were released and restored to citizenship after serving terms in Federal penitentiaries. Her sixth instance was the act of Congress providing for the direct election of Senators.

“My seventh point," said Miss Addams, “is the restoring of the ballot to men who have been disfranchised for desertion from the Army."
