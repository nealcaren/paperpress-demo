The aristocracy before 1789. Under the monarchy, the “golden branches and jade leaves” formed a useless, parasitic class of society. The nobles turned their minds to becoming masters, and each of them exercised governing authority over a large territory, with jurisdiction, the right to levy taxes, and so on. Serfdom (servage) still existed in some places, such as Nivernais, where it was redeemed by payment of a tax. The lower classes before the Revolution of 1789. In the eighteenth century, industry began to expand rapidly. This was due to the invention of machinery. In 1767 Arkwright invented the water-powered spinning machine; in 1779 Cartwright invented the automatic loom; in 1772 James Watt made the steam engine; in 1740 the method of smelting iron with coal was invented, and so on. Industry consequently developed. Factories grew larger and larger; the machines were more refined, used less power, produced more, and required fewer workers. Small factories and handicraft workshops were not thereby destroyed.

The lesser nobility consisted of people in the “unproductive professions,” living only on the income from their estates. Their inherited estates were gradually divided into tiny parcels, so that those who did not become officers in the king’s army or priests lived in difficulty. Bordeaux had only a limited means of earning a living; in 1787 the rope-making workshops employed 600 workers. In Marseille, a soap factory had 80,000 people working there. The “great works” in Lyon had 58,000 spinning workers and 9,300 weaving machines; at Anzin there were 4,000 miners. At that time wages were extremely low. They earned very little, while taxes were numerous. Rich or poor, all peasants had to pay taxes: taxes to the king, especially indirect taxes (salt, flour, and so on); the tithe (dîme) to the priests; and the tax on benefices (bénéfice) to the nobility. The three ruling orders lived in a baronial fashion, while most workers crowded together in dark, cramped places. As for living, what was it like? They too were born with the same human needs, but those miserable people had no right to enjoy anything. Their heads were swollen, their bellies bloated, and their clothes were in tatters.

On 2 June 1793, the Girondins—their leaders were natives of Gironde—the representatives of the landed and wealthy classes in the Convention nationale were overthrown and imprisoned. Before long they would go to the scaffold. In the Convention nationale, the remaining deputies were all frightened and henceforth followed the revolutionary policy of the Montagnard faction, whose leader was Robespierre. They were called Montagnards because in the assembly they sat higher than everyone else. The Montagnards had just secured a majority in the chamber, and the Commune of Paris supported them, but they too were divided. On the left was Hébert’s faction, which advocated the suppression of religion, the closing of churches, and called for terror to be spread ever more widely, while the guillotine’s blade continued to fall without pause. On the right was Danton’s faction, which advocated a policy of “humanity” and opposed the Reign of Terror. But Robespierre and his comrades, Couthon and Saint-Just, had clear evidence that Hébert and Danton were both committing shameful acts, accepting bribes and embezzling the money of the nobles and the wealthy. Robespierre resolved to eliminate his opponents. First Robespierre relied on Danton to condemn Hébert’s faction. This faction went to the scaffold one day, and, out of fear, the deputies welcomed it enthusiastically. That night, the deputies remaining on the right plotted to overthrow Robespierre. They divided up the tasks, each one assuming responsibility for a part in this drama of burying the revolutionary movement. The next day the Convention nationale assembled. Tallien, holding a dagger, mounted the rostrum and, pointing it at Robespierre’s face, accused him of being a dictator. Robespierre rose to reply, but the hostile deputies shouted noisily, so that no one could hear his words. Collot d’Herbois, seated in the chamber, kept ringing the bell, deliberately drowning out Robespierre’s voice. The uproar continued until five o’clock in the afternoon. Robespierre could not reply; he was pained to see that the people who had formerly followed him now sat impassively or joined the hostile deputies. A deputy moved that Robespierre and his friends be arrested. The motion was approved. The military commanders Robespierre, Saint-Just, Couthon, and others were taken to prison. But the men of the Commune of Paris freed them again and brought them back to the Hôtel de Ville. Hanriot, commander of the National Guard, who was also Robespierre’s friend, concentrated his troops. The Convention nationale opposed him and entrusted Deputy Barras with command of the forces loyal to the Convention; it proclaimed that many deputies were merely fronts for speculators or were in league with traitors. They waited for the situation to change. Before long the Montagnard faction fell apart; in turn, one group destroyed the other. [illegible] The great majority consisted of the surviving Girondins, the moderates, and the reactionaries, who formed an alliance to overthrow Robespierre. The conflict in the chamber was nothing other than an echo of the conflict between the classes in the country. By 1794, the French bourgeoisie no longer wanted the revolutionary movement to go any further. The social revolution had smashed the forms of production of the feudal system. From then on, industry and commerce developed very rapidly. The French bourgeoisie set about building a new regime in which it would be master. The landowners, too, yearned for social peace. They had bought the lands of the high clergy and of the émigré nobles. The Revolution brought them two things: first, political power; second, the right of ownership. What more could they want? The bourgeoisie and the landowners had achieved the aims of their class. The bourgeois revolution was complete; they did not want to proceed to a democratic revolution.

In those days, some were divided, while others were somewhat discouraged. Robespierre no longer came to the Convention and the committees as before. In the revolutionary period, every hour required the settlement of many matters of the greatest importance; his absence from several meetings also caused his influence to diminish. The reactionaries took advantage of this. They feared losing their heads. Under the law of 10 June 1794 (22 Prairial according to the revolutionary calendar), the Committee of Public Safety had the power to bring before the Revolutionary Tribunal those deputies whom it accused, without the Convention’s approval or disapproval. During the day he was absent, but at night Robespierre often came to deliver speeches at the Club des Jacobins (the club of the friends of the Constitution—Société des amis de la Constitution—which met in an old church belonging to the Jacobins). Robespierre wanted to have the traitors’ heads cut off, and the people greatly welcomed this. On 8 Thermidor (26 July), before the National Convention, Robespierre read a long speech, declaring that he would unmask the agents of the wealthy, the nobles, and the corrupt. Many deputies—some bold, others timid—feared that their own heads might soon fall. They feared even more because there were rumors that many deputies belonging to the extreme left would be prosecuted. Robespierre and his comrades were declared outside the law (that is, if arrested, they could not plead their case and would not be tried). This caused a great commotion. It happened at dusk. It was raining. The people of Paris were astonished but did not stir. The National Guard under Hanriot’s command arrived late and was not in a fighting mood. At that time, in the city hall, Robespierre and his comrades were very discouraged and could find no way out. At two o’clock in the morning, armed troops came to arrest them. Robespierre drew a pistol and tried to commit suicide. The bullet shattered his lower jaw. (According to another historian, he was shot by a soldier.) Couthon, whose legs were paralyzed, was thrown down the stairs. Hanriot was thrown out of a window and fell into the sewer. They were all arrested and taken before the Committee of Public Safety. That same day, at four o’clock in the afternoon, Robespierre and his comrades mounted the long scaffold. ... Let us examine the causes of the democratic revolution’s failure on 9 Thermidor in and outside the Convention. In the Convention, after the Girondins had been overthrown, many dissatisfied elements opposed them, but, fearing that they themselves might be implicated, they were forced to approve the policy of the Montagnards. The deputies represented the provinces, and also the bourgeoisie; the great majority did not dare oppose the Montagnards because the latter were supported by the people of Paris. The workers, on the other hand, were not satisfied. They still worried about their daily lives and continued struggling against hunger. Prices rose, yet their wages did not increase. The government also imposed high taxes, while the merchants did not hesitate to raise the prices of goods. The workers were further burdened by harsh laws, such as the law of 16 June 1791 (called the Le Chapelier Law), which prohibited strikes. In 1793, Paris, the center of the Revolution, saw wages fixed and strikes suppressed. The difficult economic situation drove artisans and small shopkeepers—those who belonged to the petty bourgeoisie and played the principal role during the revolutionary days—to despair. Many became discouraged and rebelled against hardship; they were constantly afraid for their daily lives. Five years of revolution had weakened their fighting spirit. They were tired of the black terror and tired of revolt; wanting the Revolution to succeed, for several years the people had subjected themselves to an extremely strict way of life. Every day they saw swords and severed heads, creating a heavy, oppressive atmosphere. The revolutionary spirit was gradually beaten down.
