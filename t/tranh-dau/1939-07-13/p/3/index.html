Từ 1789 đến 1793, hơn một ngân tò báo xuất bán, đù hình thúc, đù cờ lôn, nhổ. Một phàn của nguòi cách mạng, một phàn của bôn tay sai của phú hào và quý-tổc ; bôn này binh vức nhà vua, chồng lai khùng bố cách mạng.

From 1789 to 1793, more than a thousand newspapers were published, of every form and every size, large and small. Some belonged to the revolutionaries, and some to the lackeys of the wealthy and the aristocracy; the latter defended the king and opposed the revolutionary Terror.

Mãy tò báo có tên tuõi là ; Les Actes des Apôtres (cực hữu), L'ami du Peuple, cừa Marat, Le Père Duchène, cừa Hébert (cức tã), Les Révolutions de France et de Brabant, cừa Camille Desmoulins, Le Patriote français, cừa Brissot. Hai tò sau nây dúng vè phía tã.

A few newspapers of renown were: Les Actes des Apôtres (extreme right), L’Ami du Peuple, by Marat, Le Père Duchène, by Hébert (extreme left), Les Révolutions de France et de Brabant, by Camille Desmoulins, and Le Patriote français, by Brissot. The last two leaned toward the left.

Nhiều tò báo có tên la thường như L'Alambic, ou le Distillateur patriote (Nội chung rưqu hay là anh đật rưqu ai quóc), la Poule patriote (Con gà mái ai quóc), Pendez - moi, mais écoutez-moi ! (Treo có tôi Iên, nhung hay nghe tôi l) C'est incroyable (Thật khó tin), vân, vân...

Many newspapers had unusual names, such as L’Alambic, ou le Distillateur patriote (The Still, or the Patriotic Distiller), la Poule patriote (The Patriotic Hen), Pendez-moi, mais écoutez-moi ! (Hang me, but listen to me!), C’est incroyable (It is unbelievable), and so on.

Đùng về cuc tă, một tô báo duçc hoan nghinh hơn hét là L'Ami du Peuple (Bạn Dân). Mối ngày xuát băn tù 1500 tôi 2000 tô,— vi may in chay chám, —nhung mối tô duçc cã chuc nguòi dọc.

On the extreme left, the most popular newspaper of all was L’Ami du Peuple (The Friend of the People). Every day it published from 1,500 to 2,000 copies—because the printing presses ran slowly—but each copy was read by dozens of people.

2. A fighter (continued from page one) Returning to Paris, the medical doctor Marat soon made a name for himself through his talent and quickly became successful. Although he lived in comfortable and prosperous circumstances, he never forgot his convictions. In 1782, he began opposing despotic rule. In March 1789, he said: “The people can rely only on their own courage.” In April 1789, he was elected to the electoral committee in his neighborhood. In July 1789, the people of Paris stormed the Bastille; then, in September 1789, Marat published a newspaper called “The Friend of the People” (L’Ami du Peuple). From then on, in his newspaper, he passionately fought for freedom for the working masses. In July 1790, in issue 175 of “The Friend of the People,” he demanded universal suffrage. In 1791, also in “The Friend of the People,” he opposed the Le Chapelier law, because that law did not recognize workers’ right to associate or to strike. In July 1792, the French aristocrats went abroad and called on foreign countries to send troops to attack their own country and crush the Revolution. Faced with the cowardice of the bourgeoisie, Marat called on the people to arm themselves and revolt. On 1 August 1792, the king was deposed, and the Legislative Assembly proclaimed: “The fatherland is in danger.” On 9 September 1792, Marat was elected a deputy for Paris in the National Convention. On the convention floor, Marat was given a particularly warm welcome by the Jacobin deputies. On 5 April 1793, he was elected president of the Jacobin Club. At that time, the reactionary Girondins, out of jealousy, accused him of writing articles in “The Friend of the People” inciting assassination and rebellion, and demanded that he be brought before the “Revolutionary Tribunal” for trial. Thanks to the support of the Jacobin comrades and the people, and also to his eloquence, Marat was acquitted; until 2 June, when the reactionaries were driven out by the people. After fierce struggles, Marat fell ill and temporarily withdrew from political life. During that period, he was assassinated by a young woman from a wealthy family. Her name was Charlotte Corday. Marat was killed on 13 July 1793. In the newspaper “Le Père Duchesne,” Comrade Hébert wrote: “Marat is no more! People, weep for the best friend you ever had!”

THE REVOLUTIONARY ROBESPIERRE With Marat, Robespierre occupied a supreme position in the history of the Revolution. Robespierre is commonly described as the politician of the Revolution of 1789. People wish to emphasize his talent in struggles in the assembly and before the masses. … Born in 1758 at Arras (France), Robespierre belonged to the petty-bourgeois class. His mother died early, and his father abandoned him and did not raise him; by the age of eight Robespierre already knew poverty. But because he was very intelligent, the bishop of Arras granted him a scholarship to study at Louis le Grand in Paris. In 1782, after passing his examinations, he returned to Arras and took up the profession of lawyer. At that time he had absorbed the ideas of liberty of the philosophers of the eighteenth century. While practicing law he repeatedly defended the “lower classes” against the oppression and exploitation of the wealthy. On 26 April 1789, he was elected as a deputy for the Third Estate (Tiers Etat); he was then only 32 years old. Like Marat, Robespierre was a member of the Jacobin club. The Jacobins were a left-wing political organization, composed for the most part of members of the urban and rural petty bourgeoisie. Standing on a democratic platform, the Jacobins were closely allied with the workers and peasants in the struggle against the reactionaries, the Girondins, representatives of the industrial and financial bourgeoisie. At the beginning of 1792, when revolutionary France was being invaded by foreign countries, Robespierre and his Jacobin comrades called on the masses to rise up against the internal and external enemies in order to save the Revolution. The movement reached its climax on 10 August 1792, when the masses forced the Legislative Assembly to arrest King Louis XVI and bring him to trial for treason. The revolutionary army achieved a great victory over the Austrian army on 20 September 1792 at Valmy. In the election for the National Convention in August 1792, Robespierre was elected with the highest number of votes, together with Marat. In the National Convention, Robespierre displayed such powerful eloquence that the Girondins themselves were compelled to admire him.

The oppressed classes must unite in opposing the present policy of terror, demanding democratic freedom and the right to strike for the workers. Government, restore freedom to Hùm-Tiên-Quá-Long - Ganofsky- Thanh. The government must grant a general amnesty to political prisoners. In January 1793, after a vigorous speech by Robespierre, the National Convention had King Louis XVI put on trial and executed on January 21. From then until May 1793, the situation in the country became increasingly dangerous. Abroad, the coalition forces surrounded France: by May 1793, the German army had reached Mayence, the Austrian army had reached Condé and Valenciennes, the Spanish army had occupied Perpignan, the Italian army had taken Savoie, and the English army had landed at Toulon. Within the country, the counter-revolutionaries in Vendée were in revolt. Yet the Girondin government did not concern itself with saving the workers and peasants; it repressed the workers’ and peasants’ movement. Under the leadership of Robespierre and Marat, the Jacobins summoned the armed masses to rise up. On 31 May 1793, 80,000 common people surrounded the National Convention and forced it to arrest 29 Girondin deputies. From then on, Robespierre and his comrades took power and established the “Committee of the People” to terrorize the counter-revolutionaries. For several months, under Robespierre’s authority, the enemies within and without were all thrown into terror. By the end of 1793, France had emerged from the dangerous situation. From then until the middle of 1794, the reactionary forces rose up again. On 9 Thermidor, Robespierre and his Jacobin comrades were overthrown by the reactionaries in the National Convention.
